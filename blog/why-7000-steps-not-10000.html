Why 7,000 Steps Beats 10,000 (According To The Actual Research)

If you've ever owned a smartphone or a fitness tracker, you already know the number: 10,000 steps a day. It's on every app's default goal screen, printed on gym motivational posters, repeated by every fitness influencer who's ever existed. It sounds precise enough to be scientific.

It isn't. The 10,000-step target didn't come from a health study at all — it came from a marketing campaign. In 1965, a Japanese company launched a pedometer ahead of the Tokyo Olympics called the Manpo-kei, which translates literally to "10,000-steps meter." It was a catchy round number picked to sell a device, not a figure derived from research on what the human body actually needs.

So what does the research actually say?

More recent, larger studies have gone back and asked the number properly: how many daily steps are actually associated with meaningfully better health outcomes, and where do the returns start to flatten out? A 2025 review pooling 57 separate studies found that the bulk of the benefit shows up well before 10,000 — with 7,000 steps capturing nearly all of it.

7,000 steps captures nearly all the benefit of 10,000 — the last 3,000 buy you very little extra.

That's a classic diminishing-returns curve. Going from sedentary to a few thousand steps a day produces the largest jump in benefit. Each additional thousand steps after that keeps helping, but by less each time — and by the time you're past 7,000, you're deep into the flat part of the curve.

Why this matters more than it sounds like it should

None of this means 10,000 steps is bad for you — more movement is still better than less. But if you're building a daily habit you need to sustain for 75 days straight, the target you pick matters. A goal that's calibrated to actual research is one you can hit consistently and still trust that it's doing something. A goal that's really just a 60-year-old ad slogan is one you'll eventually resent, or quietly stop believing in.

This is the same principle behind every target in OriginLeap: not the roundest number, not the most Instagrammable number, but the one the evidence actually supports. That's why the app's daily step target is 7,000, not 10,000 — and why it's synced automatically from Apple Health instead of something you have to remember to log by hand.
